Planting for the Seventh Generation: On Patience and Social Change

social change

seventh generation
Reading the Seventh Generation principle as a way to think about how long social change takes, and what our part in it is.
Author

Eric Schnitger

Published

November 11, 2026

There is a principle, often attributed to the Haudenosaunee (the Iroquois Confederacy) and their Great Law of Peace, that the decisions we make today should take into account their effect on the seventh generation after us. For a long time I’ve mixed up its origins and credited it to the Menominee people of Wisconsin. I’ve since learned better, but the Menominee may be the most remarkable living example of the idea. They have harvested timber from their forest continuously since the 1850s, and today it holds more standing timber than when they began. They have taken from it for closing on two centuries. That extraction has been done with such wisdom and care that the forest after the forest has more not less.

I’ve usually understood the Seventh Generation principle as a limiting ethic of present decision and action. We should act responsibly today so the future can inherit something good. It’s a call to look ahead and to remember that our choices outlive us. All of that is true and worth remembering. Lately, though, I’ve started to notice another side of the idea, and it has interesting implications for social change.

Turning the Timeline Around

Another way of reading the principle is that the change we are working for may not arrive for seven generations and the work that must be done between now and then.

Take an enormous goal such as ending world hunger. If I approach it expecting to see it solved in my lifetime, I’ll almost certainly end up either disillusioned or exhausted, and probably both. But if I accept that I’m one participant in a project that spans decades beyond my life, the work changes character. My job is no longer to solve the problem, to finish, to accomplish. My job is to continue faithfully, doing the steps I can do now and trusting that those who come after me will carry things further than I could.

That shift takes the impossible task of achievement off my shoulders and leaves me with the very possible task of contributing. There is an old saying, often identified as a Greek proverb, that a society grows great when people plant trees whose shade they will never sit in. I think the seventh generation view goes further. We aren’t only planting for others. We’re also tending saplings someone else planted, and passing along work whose beginning we never saw and whose end we never will.

Change Without Heroes

What I find most important about this view is that it can be anti-hierarchical. A theory of change built on power usually needs a hero, a decisive moment, and a visible victory, all fitting neatly within one lifetime. That model puts a great deal of weight on the few people who hold power and very little on the many who do the ordinary work. I was often presented the story of Dr. King that way (and it still lurks around in my consciousness). Or consider the ways so many stories or movies are presented centering on an individual or small group. To tell a human sized story, that attention to the individual creates draw, empathy, attention. But does it distort our perspective around social change?

A communal, generational view of change needs none of that. It asks for slow, steady, patient work rather than brilliance. Change happens slowly with many hands, and because it’s built that way, I hope it tends to last. This view also pushes back on our habit of measuring a life by the results we can personally claim. Nobody in a seven-generation project gets to put “ended world hunger” on their résumé. And in truth, these sorts of projects towards justice are larger than seven generations, and knowing which generation to locate ourselves in takes wisdom.

Patience Is Not Passivity

I want to name a tension here, because I feel it myself. Patience about the long arc of change has to sit beside urgency about the people suffering right now. A child who is hungry today can’t be fed by the year 2200. A generational perspective is never an excuse to postpone compassion. I’m still not sure how to emotionally reconcile these things. Instead, it is often a bouncing between the anger and sorrow of suffering mixed with the centering of self for endurance.

We Are Someone’s Seventh Generation

One last thought has stayed with me. If I’m working for the seventh generation after me, then somewhere back in time someone was working for me. My generation is the future that someone else hoped for and labored toward without ever seeing it. I think of the work of abolition, liberation, and justice that have happened in history. The expansion of voting to those without property, to nonwhite people, to women.

That doesn’t mean the world is finished or that their hopes have been fully realized. Clearly they haven’t. But it does mean we’re standing on someone’s faithfulness. That seems like reason enough to pick up the work, do our share, and pass it along in good condition, the way the Menominee have done with their forest for more than a century and a half.
