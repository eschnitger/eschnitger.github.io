The Price of Work and the Worth of Work

Why does a home health aide earn less than a junior banker’s bonus? On scarcity, effective demand, and the cultural judgments built into what we pay for work.
Author

Eric Schnitger

Published

November 13, 2026

Imagine a home health aide we will never meet. She spends her mornings helping a man who has had a stroke get dressed, take his medications, and stay out of a nursing home for one more month. Somewhere across town, a junior analyst at an investment bank will earn more in his year-end bonus than she earns in a year. If I imagine a society that cared about broadly shared income and wealth, and about equal pay and outcomes across race and gender, I can’t make that ordering come out right. It looks upside down. And yet it is no accident, and I’m not even sure it is a malfunction. So the question I want to sit with is how a pay structure this far out of step with what people contribute comes to exist, and why it stays put.

The answer most of us learned in an introductory economics class is scarcity. Wages, we were told, reflect how rare a skill is relative to how much people want it. Surgeons are rare; cashiers are not. I find this argument genuinely interesting, and I think it is partly true. My suspicion is that it explains less than it seems to. It describes how prices move once a field of conditions is already in place, and it says very little about who arranged the field.

The careful version of the scarcity argument deserves its due, because it is better than the slogan. It doesn’t claim that rare things are simply expensive. It claims that a wage reflects what one more hour of a worker’s labor is worth to whoever is buying it, compared with how many other people could supply that hour. Economists call this the marginal revenue product of labor, a phrase only an economist could love. On this account, a nurse’s pay is held down by the reimbursement caps hospitals face from insurers and public programs, however scarce nurses happen to be. The hedge fund analyst, meanwhile, needn’t be a rare genius. She simply sits beside an enormous pile of capital, so a small improvement in her judgment is worth millions to someone.

Notice what has happened, though. Once we admit that wages track the value of labor to a buyer, we have already conceded that wages measure something other than contribution to the common good. A buyer’s valuation depends on what the buyer wants and on how much money the buyer has. Scarcity is never scarcity in the abstract. It is always scarcity relative to a particular arrangement of demand, and that arrangement was built out of earlier distributions of wealth, power, and esteem. If I want to understand pay, it seems I have to understand those.

Start with demand. Markets register what economists call effective demand, which is a polite way of saying wants backed by money. A need that nobody can pay for doesn’t show up as demand at all. As far as the market can see, it simply isn’t there. A wealthy household’s desire for a kitchen renovation or a private tutor counts in full, dollar for dollar. A poor neighborhood’s need for childcare, mental health services, or a bus that comes on time counts only as far as its residents, or a government acting for them, can pay. I suspect this is the deepest reason pay drifts away from contribution. The wage structure is, in part, a mirror held up to the existing distribution of wealth. Where money gathers, the work that serves money is well paid. Where need gathers without money, the work that meets that need is underpaid, underfunded, or left undone.

What unsettles me is that this is the market doing exactly what it was designed to do. It aggregates preferences weighted by purchasing power, and it does so very efficiently. That design reflects a choice about what an economy is for. Over the last several decades, the United States has increasingly treated financial return as the measure of value. Tax law has favored capital gains over wages, corporate governance has prized shareholder return, and public goods like a sturdy safety net, universal childcare, or well-funded schools have been treated as costs to trim. Under those priorities, work that produces social goods without producing a revenue stream will be paid less. Teachers, social workers, public defenders, and Medicaid-funded care workers are paid out of budgets, which means their wages are policy decisions. Somebody, somewhere, decided this is what their work is worth to us.

A related pattern has to do with where a job sits. Some jobs sit beside great rivers of money, and it turns out that dipping a small cup into a very large river still fills a very large bucket. Corporate lawyers, investment bankers, private equity partners, and senior executives stand where enormous sums flow past. Their pay seems to reflect the size of what they touch more than the size of what they make.

Compare a teacher. Over a lifetime, a good teacher’s contribution may be immense: higher earnings for her students, better health, more participation in civic life. For all that, her contribution is diffuse. It is spread across decades and thousands of people, it is hard to attribute to her, and it never arrives as a revenue stream an employer can capture and share. The teacher creates value that society absorbs. The banker handles value that can be skimmed. The market, being a market, rewards the banker.

The supply side of the story turns out to be less natural than it looks, too. Many high-paying professions are scarce partly because entry is deliberately restricted. Licensing, credential requirements, limited training slots, and immigration rules all shape how many people may do a given job. The number of medical residency positions in the United States has long been held down by federal funding rules, which in turn limits how many physicians the country produces. Bar exams and professional accreditation do similar work in other fields. Some of this guards real goods like public safety. Some of it mostly guards incomes. I don’t know where the line falls in every case, and I doubt anyone does. Either way, scarcity here is at least partly made by institutions. It is worth noticing, too, that the jobs with the fewest such protections, home care and food service and farm labor among them, are disproportionately done by women, immigrants, and workers of color. The power to manufacture scarcity is itself unevenly shared.

Of everything here, the evidence that most convinces me that pay carries cultural judgment comes from the sociologist Paula England and her work on the devaluation of women’s work. The basic pattern is well established. Occupations with a higher share of women pay less than those with fewer women, even after accounting for the education and skill the jobs require. The question is why.

Two stories compete. The queuing story says pay drives gender composition: when a job’s pay or conditions decline, men leave, and women, who often face narrower options, fill the gap. The devaluation story runs the other way. When a job fills with women, employers and the wider culture start to see the work as less skilled and less valuable, and pay follows that perception downward.

In 2009, Asaf Levanon, Paula England, and Paul Allison tested these stories in Social Forces using U.S. Census data from 1950 to 2000. Because they could follow the same occupations over time, they could watch what happened to pay as each occupation’s gender mix changed. They found substantial support for devaluation and very little for queuing. As women moved into an occupation, its pay tended to fall afterward. Coverage of the study pointed to recreation work and ticket agents, both of which shifted from mostly male to mostly female and saw their relative wages drop sharply. Computer programming tells the same story in reverse. It began as clerical work largely done by women, and as it became male-dominated and prestigious, the pay rose with it.

Observational data can never entirely rule out a hidden third factor, and I want to be careful here. A 2026 survey experiment by England, Levanon, and colleagues took that worry seriously. They described the same occupation to different respondents, changing only its gender composition. When people were told a job was mostly done by women, they recommended lower pay for it than when they were told it was mixed or mostly male. The work was identical. Only its association with women changed. I can’t think of a cleaner demonstration that wages carry cultural judgments.

The devaluation seems to reach beyond gender to the kind of work itself. England and others have documented a care penalty: jobs that involve caring for people pay less than their skill demands would predict, whether men or women do them. Care is coded as feminine, as something done out of love or nature, and so as something that shouldn’t need to be paid well. That coding carries a racial history as well. Domestic and agricultural labor, much of it done by Black women and immigrants, was deliberately left out of early New Deal labor protections, and the echo of that exclusion still sounds in how we pay for care.

Where does this leave the scarcity argument? Somewhere smaller, I think. Scarcity operates, but it operates inside a field already shaped by who holds wealth, whose needs count as demand, which jobs sit near the money, which professions can close their doors, and which kinds of work a culture has learned to discount. A defender of markets could reasonably answer that these are distortions to be corrected, and that markets still gather information better than any planner could. I take that answer seriously. It also seems to concede the point I care about. The wage structure looks much less like a neutral measurement of value and much more like a record of social choices about whose work matters. If that is right, it could be chosen differently. Whether we have the will to choose differently is a question I can’t answer here.
