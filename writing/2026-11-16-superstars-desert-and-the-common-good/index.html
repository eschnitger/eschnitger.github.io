Superstars, Desert, and the Common Good

economics
wages
inequality
meritocracy
luck
philosophy
social change
Sherwin Rosen explains how small differences in talent become enormous differences in pay at the top. Michael Sandel asks whether anyone deserves them, and what a society that thinks they do loses.
Author

Eric Schnitger

Published

November 16, 2026

Paula England’s research left me with an uncomfortable picture of the bottom and middle of the wage structure. Work associated with women and with care is paid less because of whom it is associated with, more than because of what it asks of the people doing it. The top of the wage structure has its own distortion, though I’ve come to think of it as a cousin of the first. Up there, small differences in talent, timing, and fortune get stretched into enormous differences in income. The economist Sherwin Rosen helps explain how that stretching happens. The philosopher Michael Sandel helps me think about why it matters.

In 1981, Rosen, a labor economist at the University of Chicago, published “The Economics of Superstars” in the American Economic Review. He was puzzling over something anyone can see in entertainment, sports, and some professions. A handful of people earn extraordinary sums while most of their peers earn ordinary livings, even when the gap in talent between them looks slim. Why should a slightly better singer earn a hundred times more than a very good one?

Part of Rosen’s answer is that lesser talent is often a poor substitute for greater talent. Listening to ten mediocre singers is not the same as listening to one great one, and I wouldn’t want two competent surgeons splitting a risky operation if one excellent surgeon were available. When buyers want the best and can’t make up for quality with quantity, demand piles onto whoever ranks highest, even if the margin is narrow.

The rest of his answer concerns what he called joint consumption technology, a dry name for a striking idea. Some services can be delivered to a huge audience at almost no extra cost to the person providing them. A performer works roughly as hard whether a recording reaches a thousand listeners or ten million. A textbook author writes the book once, however many students are assigned it. When that is true, the best performer isn’t confined to a local market. She can serve nearly everyone at once.

Put those together and the relationship between talent and earnings bends sharply upward. Economists call it convex; I picture a ski jump. The top performer captures a disproportionate share of a very large market, the runner-up captures far less, and everyone further down competes over the crumbs. Rosen noticed that radio, recordings, and television made this worse by enlarging the audience any one person could reach. A nineteenth-century tenor was limited by the size of the opera house. A recording artist has no such ceiling.

Rosen offered all this as explanation, not critique. He was a fairly orthodox Chicago economist, and he read superstar markets as efficient responses to what consumers want. Still, the model carries an implication he didn’t press. If small differences in quality produce vast differences in income, then the size of a superstar’s paycheck tells us very little about how much more she contributes than the people just beneath her.

The economists who came after Rosen kept tugging at that thread, and each tug seemed to make luck and structure loom larger. In 1985, Moshe Adler argued that superstars could emerge even if there were no differences in talent at all. Enjoying a performer, he pointed out, is easier and more fun when other people already know her, because then you can talk about her, share references, and belong to something. Popularity snowballs. A performer who gets an early lead, perhaps by sheer chance, becomes more valuable to each new fan precisely because other fans already exist. On Adler’s reading, who becomes the superstar may be close to arbitrary. That connects to something from the first essay: attention itself can manufacture the scarcity that superstars then capture.

Robert Frank and Philip Cook carried Rosen’s logic beyond entertainment in The Winner-Take-All Society (1995). They saw the same structure spreading through law, finance, medicine, academia, and publishing, and they worried about waste. Too many talented people get drawn into lottery-like contests for a few enormous prizes, while useful but unglamorous work goes undersupplied. The prizes in these contests look less like wages and more like rents paid for rank.

Xavier Gabaix and Augustin Landier, writing in 2008, used a version of the same logic to explain executive pay. A CEO’s decisions ripple through an entire firm, so a slightly more capable CEO is worth far more to a giant firm than to a small one. As American firms grew enormously after 1980, they argued, CEO pay grew alongside them. Critics answer that captured boards and executive bargaining power explain much of the same rise, and I find that critique persuasive. Even on Gabaix and Landier’s own terms, though, a CEO’s pay depends heavily on how big a firm she happens to lead. That is a fact about structure. It isn’t a virtue.

The further economists looked into superstar markets, then, the more they found outcomes hanging on structure, timing, network effects, and luck. Talent matters, of course. It is simply magnified, sorted, and rewarded by forces the talented person didn’t create.

Rosen helps explain how superstar incomes arise. Sandel asks whether the people who receive them deserve them, and what happens to a society that is sure they do.

In Justice: What’s the Right Thing to Do? (2009), Sandel treats justice as a matter of giving people what they are due, which requires us to decide which virtues and ways of life a society should honor. When he takes up libertarianism, he uses Michael Jordan’s enormous earnings as a test case. The libertarian view, building on Robert Nozick, holds that Jordan is entitled to whatever fans freely pay to watch him play. Sandel then brings in John Rawls to complicate things. Jordan did not earn his natural gifts, which Rawls would call morally arbitrary. It was a further piece of luck that he lived in a society that happened to prize basketball and to pay lavishly for it. Born in another century, with the same body and the same drive, he might have been a very good farmer.

Rosen’s model gives Sandel’s point some mechanical teeth. Jordan’s income depended on television that expanded basketball’s audience, on a convex reward structure, and on a culture that had decided basketball was something to care about. Adler goes further still, since snowballing attention may help decide which athletes become global icons in the first place.

In The Tyranny of Merit (2020), Sandel turns from individual desert to meritocracy as an ideology. His target is the widespread belief that market rewards track what people deserve, so that the successful earned their success and the struggling earned their struggle. He thinks this belief does double damage. It breeds hubris among the winners, who credit themselves alone, and humiliation among those left behind, who are told in effect that their low pay reflects their low worth.

What I find striking is that the great defenders of market society didn’t believe market rewards measured merit either. Friedrich Hayek separated the market value of a person’s work from that person’s moral merit quite sharply. Frank Knight observed that satisfying consumer wants is a different thing from doing something ethically good. Rawls held that talents themselves are morally arbitrary. Sandel’s own example is pointed. A casino magnate may earn vastly more than a teacher, and we can accept that the market pays the magnate more without believing the magnate deserves more. His worry is that even Hayek’s and Rawls’s alternatives still let success confer social esteem, and so they never quite escape the hubris they reject. Superstar markets only sharpen the problem. When the gap between winners and everyone else is enormous, so is the temptation to read it as a gap in worth.

Sandel’s constructive proposal is what he calls contributive justice, which draws on Aristotle, the civic republican tradition, and Catholic social teaching. Distributive justice asks how income should be shared. Contributive justice asks whether people have a real chance to be recognized and esteemed for producing what others need and value. For Sandel, the dignity of work, being seen as someone whose labor serves the common good, matters as much as the paycheck.

This is where luck moves to the center. If success owes a great deal to luck, then the winners have reason for humility and gratitude, and those who lose have reason not to accept the market’s verdict on their worth. Sandel makes the idea concrete with a proposal for elite college admissions: a lottery among qualified applicants, meant to make luck visible and to deflate the self-congratulation of the admitted. He also suggests shifting the tax burden away from labor and toward consumption, wealth, and financial speculation, and using public money to support the wages of workers whose contributions the market undervalues.

His critics have fair objections. Some say he never really explains what makes some work civically important and other work not. Others point out, with a certain glee, that his remedies are taxes and wage subsidies, which still use money as the measure of recognition he criticizes. An economist in Rosen’s tradition would add that even undeserved superstar pay does useful work, steering talent toward those who value it most. Sandel’s reply is that efficiency can’t settle the question, because pay structures teach a society what it honors. I’m not sure that reply fully answers the economist. And yet I find myself agreeing with Sandel more than not. Read together, Rosen and Sandel suggest that the top of the wage structure, like the bottom, owes less to merit than we like to pretend, and that admitting the role of luck may be where a fairer account of contribution has to begin.
