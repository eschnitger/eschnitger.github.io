The Invisible Hand of Luck

economics
inequality

meritocracy

psychology

Luck is one of the central causes of economic success, yet averages and hero stories both hide it, leaving underpaid workers to read the market’s devaluation as a verdict on themselves.
Author

Eric Schnitger

Published

November 18, 2026

The first two essays came at the wage structure from opposite ends and, somewhat to my surprise, arrived at the same place. At the bottom and in the middle, pay carries cultural judgments about care work and women’s work, a demand that counts money instead of need, and a supply shaped by whoever can restrict it. At the top, superstar dynamics stretch small differences in talent and timing into vast differences in income, and the winners tend to mistake structural fortune for personal merit. Underneath both, I keep finding the same thing, and American culture is strangely reluctant to name it. It is luck. I want to suggest that luck is one of the central causes of economic success, far more than a footnote to it. I also want to ask why it is so hard to see, especially for the people it affects most. My hunch is that the two ways we usually tell economic stories, through statistical averages and through individual heroes, both hide luck, and that ordinary workers are left to absorb the devaluation described in the earlier essays as if it were a verdict on who they are.

Start with the largest fact I know of. The economist Branko Milanovic has estimated that the country a person is born in explains roughly 60 percent of the variation in income across the world’s population, and that parents’ income position explains much of the rest. Effort, talent, and everything else share what remains. Nobody chooses a country of birth or a set of parents. By this measure, the most important thing determining a person’s income is a lottery held before she takes her first breath. I find that a dizzying thought.

Within the United States the lottery keeps running. Raj Chetty and his colleagues found that the share of American children who grow up to out-earn their parents fell from about 90 percent for those born in 1940 to about 50 percent for those born in the 1980s. Most of that decline traces to how unequally growth was shared, more than to how slowly the economy grew. Whether a child does better than her parents now seems to depend a great deal on when she was born and where her family sits in the distribution. A child born in the 1940s entered an economy that spread its gains widely. A child born in the 1980s entered one that gathered them at the top. Neither child had any say.

The earlier essays add more layers. Whether your skills are in demand depends on which needs come with money attached. Whether your occupation pays well depends on its closeness to capital, its ability to restrict entry, and whether the culture codes it as women’s work. Whether you become a superstar depends on network effects, timing, and the snowballing of attention Moshe Adler described. At every turn, things outside a person’s control are shaping the reward. Effort matters. It just operates on a field whose slope was set by fortune.

Robert Frank, whose work on winner-take-all markets came up in the second essay, later wrote Success and Luck (2016) to make this case directly. In crowded contests full of talented people, he argued, the winner is usually someone who is both very talented and very lucky, because talent alone can’t separate the top contenders. The more competitive the field, the more luck decides who wins. That ought to give anyone at the top of a crowded field a moment’s pause.

If luck is so central, why do we see it so poorly? Part of the answer, I suspect, lies in how economic knowledge reaches most of us. We tend to meet it as averages. We hear that college graduates earn more than high school graduates, that each extra year of schooling raises lifetime earnings, that some majors pay better than others. These are true statements about populations. By the time they reach the dinner table, though, they have turned into statements about choices: go to college, pick the right major, work hard, and you will earn more.

Averages wash out luck almost by design. The average return to a degree blends the graduate who entered a booming labor market with the one who graduated into a recession, the one with family connections with the one without, the one whose field took off with the one whose field folded. The variance, which is precisely where luck lives, disappears behind a single tidy number. What’s left looks like a dependable exchange rate between effort and reward.

For someone whose life falls below the average, that framing can be cruel. If the average says education pays, and your education didn’t, the obvious conclusion is that something must be wrong with you. The structure that sorted you, whether it was graduating in 2009, entering a feminized occupation, or living in a region whose main employer left town, vanishes from view. The average starts to sound like an accusation.

We also meet economic knowledge as stories about individuals. American culture is saturated with tales of exceptional people who made it through sheer grit, the founder in the garage, the athlete who outworked everyone. Michael Jordan is something like the patron saint of the genre. The familiar version says he was left off his high school varsity team as a sophomore and answered with such relentless work that he became the greatest player of his generation.

I don’t think the story is false. I think it is partial. As the second essay argued with help from Rosen and Sandel, Jordan also had rare physical gifts he didn’t create, arrived just as television and global marketing were turning basketball into a worldwide spectacle, and lived in a culture that had decided basketball was worth caring about. His work ethic was real. So was his luck. Luck just doesn’t make for a good locker-room speech, so it drops out of the telling.

Hero stories also suffer from a survivorship problem. We hear from the person who worked relentlessly and won. We rarely hear from the many who worked just as hard and didn’t. Winner-take-all markets produce a few enormous winners and a crowd of near-misses, so the people we see are almost entirely those for whom effort and luck happened to line up. The lesson most of us absorb is that effort produces success. A truer lesson might be that effort is necessary and nowhere near sufficient.

So the average and the hero fail in complementary ways. The average hides luck by erasing variation. The hero hides luck by showing us only the lucky. Between them, an ordinary person has very few cultural tools for seeing how luck has shaped her own life.

Our own psychology doesn’t help. Shai Davidai and Thomas Gilovich have described what they call the headwinds and tailwinds asymmetry. We notice the obstacles we’ve had to push through, vividly and in detail, and we barely register the advantages that carried us along. A cyclist feels the headwind on every pedal stroke and forgets the tailwind the moment it’s behind her. In economic life, this means we remember our struggles and treat our advantages as the natural backdrop to our effort. For the fortunate, the result is the meritocratic hubris Sandel describes, a sincere but mistaken conviction that one’s place was simply earned. For the less fortunate, something like what the psychologist Melvin Lerner called the belief in a just world can turn inward. If the world is basically fair, and I’m struggling, perhaps I deserve to be.

This is where the threads of all three essays meet, I think. Picture a home health aide, a preschool teacher, or a social worker. Her work bears every mark described in the first essay. It meets needs that don’t come with money attached, it sits far from capital, it lacks the protections that create scarcity, and it is coded as women’s work and devalued accordingly. Her low pay is largely a product of those structures and of where her luck placed her within them. And yet the stories available to her hide nearly all of this. The averages tell her that better choices would have meant better pay. The heroes tell her that harder work would have lifted her out. Her own mind tells her the headwinds are hers to beat. The meritocratic culture Sandel criticizes tells her that pay reflects worth. She takes a double injury, devalued first by the market and the culture, and then invited to read that devaluation as a judgment on herself. The fortunate professional faces the mirror image. He reads his salary as a measure of his contribution, doesn’t see the structures that lifted him, and concludes that the people below him had the same chances and made worse use of them.

None of this means effort and agency don’t count. People make real choices, and those choices matter. What I’m after is a more honest account, one in which effort operates inside structures that decide how much effort is rewarded, and in which those structures are handed out by fortune and kept in place by social choices.

Such an account would carry moral weight. Sandel draws on an old tension between grace and works, between the sense that what we have is partly a gift and the conviction that we have earned all of it. A society that remembered grace would have reason for humility at the top and for solidarity across the whole. It could see that the care worker’s low pay says more about the wage structure than about her, and that the executive’s high pay says more about the structure than about him. That recognition seems to me to be the ground of contributive justice, a commitment to honoring work for what it gives to the common good, whatever the market happens to pay for it.

Someone defending the present arrangement might answer that dwelling on luck saps motivation, and that people work harder when they believe effort pays. Frank offers a partial reply: people reminded of their luck tend to become more generous, and he finds little reason to think they become lazier. I’m not certain that settles it. I am fairly sure, though, that a culture which tells itself untrue stories about luck doesn’t make anyone more deserving. It only makes the fortunate more comfortable and the unfortunate more ashamed. Telling the truth about luck might be where a more just account of what work is worth begins, though I suspect the road from there is a long one.
